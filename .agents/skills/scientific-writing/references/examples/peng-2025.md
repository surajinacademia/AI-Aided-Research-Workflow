#### Abstract

Biological tissues exhibit sharp phase transitions where cells collectively transition from disordered to ordered states at critical densities. We demonstrate through bio-chemomechanical modeling that this emergent behavior arises from a nonmonotonic dependence on nonlinear extracellular matrix (ECM) mechanics: mechanical communication between cells is optimized at intermediate stiffness values where cells can both generate sufficient forces and create strain-stiffened tension bands in the ECM. This balance establishes a critical cell spacing threshold for cell-cell communication ( $\sim 100$ to $200 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ ) that is conserved across experimental observations for a broad range of cell types and collagen densities. Our model reveals that the critical stretch ratio at which fibrous networks transition from compliant to strain-stiffening governs this threshold through the formation of tension bands between neighboring cells. These mechanical communication networks drive collective phase transition in tissue condensation when cell density exceeds an effective percolation threshold. Our model explains how microscale cell-ECM interactions control emergent mechanical properties in biological systems and offers insight both into the physics of inhomogeneous materials under active stress, and into potential mechanical interventions for wound healing and fibrotic disorders.

The emergence of collective cellular behavior is fundamental to biological processes ranging from embryonic development to wound healing. In many physical systems, local interactions give rise to global ordering through collective phase transitions. In biological tissues, cells embedded in fibrous extracellular matrix (ECM) undergo sharp transitions from disordered to ordered states at critical cell densities ( $1-5$ ), representing a key example of how mechanical interactions alone can coordinate multicellular systems.

This mechanical coordination manifests dramatically in reconstituted tissue constructs (5-7), model systems with cells embedded in purified fibrous matrix proteins that allow precise control over cell density and ECM properties (8, 9). Doha et al. (5) characterize this phase transition as a function of initial cell spacing, showing that cells spaced sufficiently close together coordinate to compress the hydrogel to a small fraction of its initial volume, while cells beyond this critical distance fail to produce significant compaction. Marquez et al. (6) report a critical cell density of $\phi_{c}=400,000$ to 500,000 cells $/ \mathrm{mL}$ for chick embryo fibroblasts in $1 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{mL}$ type I collagen, corresponding to a spacing $s_{c}=1 / \phi_{c}^{1 / 3}$ of 130 to $140 \mu \mathrm{~m}$. Fernandez and Bausch observe a critical area density of 0.0001 to 0.0002 cells $/ \mu \mathrm{m}^{2}$ in $250 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ thick gels ( $\phi_{c}=400,000$ to 800,000 cells $/ \mathrm{mL}$ ) for MC3T3-E1 osteoblast cells in $2.4 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{mL}$ collagen, yielding $s_{c}=110$ to $140 \mu \mathrm{~m}$. Liu et al. (4) term the phenomenon "paratensile signaling," and report a critical spacing of $\sim 200 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ within which activated cells of the human hepatic cell line LX-2 can activate neighboring quiescent LX-2 cells in $2 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{mL}$ type I collagen. The most comprehensive characterization, by Doha et al. (5), demonstrated that contraction begins at 250,000 NIH 3T3 fibroblast cells $/ \mathrm{mL}$ and increases to an asymptote at $2,000,000$ cells $/ \mathrm{mL}$, establishing a critical spacing range of $s_{c}=79$ to $160 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ in $2 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{mL}$ collagen (4). This universality extends beyond fibroblasts to neuronal connectivity (10), suggesting underlying physical principles that transcend cell type. The critical spacing threshold bears hallmarks of percolation phenomena. However, a fundamental paradox remains: while individual cells exhibit contractile responses that increase monotonically with substrate stiffness, collective behaviors exhibit sharp transitions characteristic of critical phenomena $(11,12)$.

## Significance

This study reveals how cell-to-cell mechanotransduction through extracellular matrix (ECM) determines outcomes in a wide range of physiological and pathological processes. Using bio-chemo-mechanical methods, we dissect how cell spacing and shape interact with nonlinear ECM mechanics to determine how cells influence the contractility and polarization of their neighbors. This provides a mechanistic explanation for the concepts of optimal ECM rigidity and paratensile signaling. In addition to filling a critical gap in our understanding of cellular interactions in fibrosis and wound healing, results offer insights into how cell spreading facilitates long-range force transmission within tissues in disease and health, and offer insight into how manipulating the physical microenvironment may enable more effective treatments for fibrosis, cancer progression, and chronic wounds.

[^0]number of theoretical frameworks have been developed. A class of models that has been particularly impactful represents cells as orientation-polarizable force dipoles, in analogy with dielectric polarization (13). These approaches reveal that cell force polarization depends critically on ECM rigidity and that ellipsoidal cells exhibit shape-dependent polarization patterns (14). Subsequent work examining individual cells established the concept of optimal ECM stiffness, with actomyosin contractility exhibiting maximal polarization at an intermediate ECM stiffness that is sensitive to cell shape anisotropy (15). Optimal stiffness affects cell migration as well, and emerges from a competition between spreading-induced polarization driven by anisotropic stretching at the cell periphery, and self-polarization arising from the cytoskeleton's response to its own contractile stresses (1518). A central outcome of this work is that nonlinear strainstiffening properties of biological matrices fundamentally alter cell-cell interactions, enhancing the range of cell-generated force transmission and enabling traction-induced fiber reorganization that further extends mechanical communication distances (1923). However, the features of ECM behavior most critical to these phenomena have not been identified, and the role of recursive feedback between cell and ECM polarization is unclear.

To uncover these mechanisms driving critical transitions, we used an integrated bio-chemo-mechanical approach that captures recursive interactions between cell contractility and ECM mechanics. Our model combines two key components: first, a stress-responsive cellular model that accounts for mechanochemical feedback driving myosin motor density and polarization (24-26); and second, a fibrous ECM model that captures straindependent fiber recruitment, alignment, and stiffening, while accounting for the distinct responses of collagen to tension (stiffening) versus compression (buckling) (27-29).

This integrated model reveals how cellular spacing determines the formation of mechanical tension bands between cells, establishing a threshold distance below which cells can effectively polarize their neighbors. It also demonstrates that the emergence of mechanical cross-talk in tissue remodeling depends crucially on the critical stretch ratio, $\lambda_{c}$, at which collagen fibers transition from compliant to strain-stiffening behavior. Our approach provides a quantitative physical model for understanding how nonlinear elasticity controls phase transitions in active biological materials, and results suggest potential targets for therapeutic intervention in fibrotic disorders through manipulation of ECM mechanics.

This evolution accounted for chemo-mechanical feedback through a "chemo-mechanical feedback parameter," $\alpha$, representing the tendency to increase contractility in response to mechanical stress. It also accounted for resistance to this increase in metabolic output, represented by the "chemical stiffness parameter," $\beta$. Results revealed mechanisms by which cells can activate their neighbors through mechanical interactions. As expected from numerous earlier studies ( $4,5,10,30-32$ ), even spherical cells in sufficiently close proximity stretched and aligned the collagen between them (Fig. $2 D$ and $E$ ). Bands of collagen with fibers aligned along the contractile axis between cells, and buckling perpendicular to this, were evident from plots of the ratio between the maximum and minimum principal stretch ratios $\left(\lambda_{1} / \lambda_{3}\right)$ (Fig. $2 E$ ) (28, 33). The spherical cells compacted regions of ECM around them to levels beyond the critical stretch, taken here to be $\lambda_{c}=1.04$, as is appropriate for collagen in the 1 to $2 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{mL}$ range (20, 28) (Fig. 2D).

These effects were recursive, with the alignment and stiffening of the ECM leading to increasing activation of cell contractility, and cell contractility leading to further alignment of the ECM (34). To quantify these effects, we considered how cell spacing affected two key parameters describing the cell response (Fig. $2 B$ and $C$ ): the steady state density of myosin motors within the cell, $\tilde{\rho}=\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) / 3$, and the steady state polarization of myosin motors within the cell, $\left(\tilde{\eta}=\left(\rho_{1}-\rho_{3}\right) / 2\right)$, where $\rho_{1}$ and $\rho_{3}$ are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of $\rho$, respectively. The density of myosin motors, $\tilde{\rho}$, increased very slightly below the critical spacing (Fig. 2 $C$ ). However, the polarization of myosin motors, $\tilde{\eta}$ changed dramatically at the critical spacing (Fig. 2B). This alignment of actomyosin machinery is analogous to reports of cells in close proximity changing shape and extending protrusions toward each other ( 5,35 ), and was associated with formation of strain-stiffened tension bands between cells (Fig. 2D).

This is consistent with the physical expectation that free boundaries provide less resistance for cell traction, reducing the mechanical feedback that drives polarization.

Myosin Activation Is Determined by ECM Properties Alone, While Cell Polarization Also Depends Upon Cell Spacing. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying these observations, we studied the joint effects on paratensile signaling of the cell spacing, $s / R$, and the prestiffening ECM modulus, $E^{b}$. We again studied
spherical cells so as to capture the first stages of cell activation. As $E^{b}$ increased, the zone of strain stiffening around the cells decreased and the tension band between them disappeared (Fig. 3A). However, despite this diminishing alignment of ECM fibers, cell activation (motor density, $\tilde{\rho}$ ) increased monotonically and substantially with $E^{b}$ (Fig. 3B). The effect of cell spacing on $\tilde{\rho}$ was only a few percent, and extremely small compared to the variation with respect to $E^{b}$ (Fig. 3D).

The effect of stiffness on myosin motor polarization $\tilde{\eta}$ was much more substantial, with polarization disappearing beyond a critical spacing that diminished with increasing stiffness $E^{b}$ (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The peak value of myosin motor polarization $\tilde{\eta}$ was determined by a trade-off between having a modulus sufficiently low for overlapping regions of ECM alignment to connect the cells, but sufficiently high to have substantial myosin motor activation, $\tilde{\rho}$ (Fig. 3E). As the baseline ECM modulus increased from 0.01 to 100 kPa , the critical spacing, defined as the threshold below which myosin polarization rises sharply, decreased. This relationship can be understood as an interplay between ECM stiffness, cell contraction, and strain stiffening. In softer matrices (top, low crosslinking of collagen), cells create relatively large deformation zones that overlap, but the ECM generates insufficient resistance force to trigger strong chemo-mechanical feedback, resulting in lower cell activation and lower transduction of force by neighboring cells. In stiffer matrices (bottom, high crosslinking of collagen) self-activation by cells can be high, but deformation of the ECM is low, so that signals are not passed to neighboring cells. At intermediate levels, cells can both exert a substantial force and project it over longer distances because of sufficient ECM stiffness and focusing of force into tension bands via strain stiffening.

The exquisite sensitivity of motor polarization $\tilde{\eta}$ to paratensile signaling, and the relative insensitivity of motor density $\tilde{\rho}$, can be understood through tension bands in the ECM. These bands create mechanical communication channels that have high stiffness but that occupy only a small fraction of a cell's total ECM interface. Consequently, the volume-averaged mechanical environment, which governs motor density through mean stress, changes only slightly. In contrast, mechanical anisotropy created by tension bands drives strong motor polarization. This differential sensitivity of motor density versus polarization suggests that cells possess two distinct mechanosensing systems: one that detects mechanical anisotropy to orient cellular machinery $(15,34)$ and another that integrates overall mechanical cues to set baseline contractility. This dual sensing mechanism enables the sharp phase transition observed experimentally, as small changes in cell spacing can trigger large changes in directional force transmission without requiring proportional changes in total force generation.

The finding also suggests that therapeutic strategies targeting overall ECM stiffness may be less effective than those targeting mechanical anisotropy or directional stiffening in controlling tissue organization.

Disruption of a Mechanical Feedback Loop Can Halt Fibrosis Progression. The parameter $\lambda_{c}$ is a feature of the fibrous architecture of the ECM and can be modulated in tissue constructs by varying factors such as collagen pore diameter, collagen concentration, collagen crosslinking (e.g., by glycation), and polymerization temperature (28, 35). In liver fibrosis, for example, advanced glycation end-products enhance collagen crosslinking (27), which reduces $\lambda_{c}$ and alters long-range force transmission. These processes are also associated with changes in the baseline Young's modulus of the ECM. We sought to understand how ECM properties affect motor polarization by varying these parameters in the two-cell model.

These trends were robust against the parameters of the integrated cell-ECM mechanobiological feedback model (SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3), and against cell spacing (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This might provide insight into why efforts to reduce ECM stiffness in fibrosis using by upregulation of MMPs have not proven successful (37): changes on the order of a factor of two to $E^{b}$ have little effect on $\tilde{\rho}$. However, relatively small changes to $\lambda_{c}$, especially if coupled with changes to $E^{b}$, can have a much larger effect.

We explored how varying $\lambda_{c}$ might affect paratensile signaling (Fig. $4 C-E$ ). When strain-stiffening is pronounced ( $\lambda_{c}=1.04$ ), cells could recruit and align ECM fibers into tension bands even at moderate cell spacings and the sharp transition in motor polarization occurs. Progressively increasing $\lambda_{c}$ resulted in less ECM strain stiffening, reducing cell-cell mechanical coupling. For infinite $\lambda_{c}$, corresponding to an isotropic hyperelastic ECM, motor polarization was negligible regardless of cell spacing, and overall motor density was reduced dramatically. These results highlight that the nonlinear, strain-stiffening properties of fibrous ECM are essential for enabling the critical spacing phenomenon. Without fiber recruitment and alignment, the hallmarks of biological matrices like collagen, cells lose their ability to coordinate mechanically over distances relevant to tissue-scale processes.

Cell Spreading Enhances Mechanical Feedback between Cells and ECM. Because these results revealed a key role for actomyosin polarity, we next studied the polarization of shape that is characteristic of cell activation and spreading. An isolated cell with an aspect ratio of $b / a=2$ in a very large ECM was modeled, with all parameters set at the baseline level except the ECM modulus $E^{b}$. Volume was kept constant so that $a^{2} b=R^{3}$. As with spherical cells, the zone around the cell for which the critical stretch ratio was exceeded shrank with increasing ECM modulus $E^{b}$ (Fig. 5A). The displacement field was again a strong function of $E^{b}$ (Fig. $5 B$ ). The myosin motor density $\tilde{\rho}$ was again a strong function of $E^{b}$, but only weakly dependent on $b / a$ (Fig. 5C). Motor polarization $\tilde{\eta}$ was a much stronger function of modulus, $E^{b}$, with higher aspect ratio cells having higher motor polarization. The optimal stiffness for motor polarization decreased slightly with increasing aspect ratio (Fig. 5D).

Both mean motor density ( $\tilde{\rho}$ ) and mean motor polarization $(\tilde{\eta})$ increased as cells became more elongated (Fig. $6 B$ and $C)$. This enhancement in myosin motor activity coincided with higher curvature at the ends of elongated cells, which creates localized stress concentrations. These stress concentrations, in turn, triggered a bio-chemo-mechanical feedback loop within the cell, leading to increased overall contractility and motor density.

The model also demonstrated increased force transmission by elongated cells. Both the first principal stress and displacement were transmitted further by cells with higher aspect ratios (Fig. $6 D$ and $E$ ), potentially facilitating enhanced cell-cell communication through the ECM.

The spatial anisotropy of stress in elongated cells led to increased expression of phosphorylated myosin light chain and F-actin at the cell ends. This localized upregulation resulted in higher motor polarization, further contributing to the cell's exertion and transmission of forces. The strain-stiffening properties of the ECM amplified this effect, as the larger strains induced by elongated cells lead to increased local ECM stiffness.

## Discussion

Our results reveal that the critical cell spacing threshold emerges from a nonmonotonic relationship between ECM stiffness and collective behavior (Fig. 7). Polarization, the key parameter governing collective behavior, exhibits an optimum at intermediate ECM stiffness (Fig. $3 C$ and $E$ ), analogous to linear elastic models (15). This optimum arises from coupled requirements: cells must generate sufficient contractile forces
(which increases with stiffness) while retaining the ability to deform ECM into tension bands as mechanical communication channels (which decreases with stiffness). The phase transition's sharpness reflects this narrow parameter window where both requirements are simultaneously satisfied, and reveals why pathological changes that either soften or stiffen the ECM can disrupt normal tissue function.

The critical spacing represents a mechanical percolation limit where cell-generated tension bands interconnect to coordinate collective behavior. These bands can form when ECM stretches beyond $\lambda_{c}$, triggering strain-stiffening that creates high-stiffness communication channels (20,28). Cell elongation extends this critical spacing by generating stress concentrations that penetrate deeper into ECM, effectively increasing each cell's reach (10, 24), and providing a mechanism by which fibroblast activation can actively modulate mechanical communication range.

While the mechanism appears universal, the specific critical spacing value depends on both cellular and ECM parameters. During fibrosis, multiple parameters change synergistically: Increased crosslinking raises $\lambda_{c}$, excess ECM deposition increases $E^{f} / E^{b}$, and proliferation reduces cell spacing. Our model reveals how these changes act together, with altered mechanics reducing the critical spacing just as increased density pushes tissues past this threshold, accelerating the switch-like progression of fibrosis and suggesting a reason why single-parameter interventions often fail.

These predictions align with extensive experimental evidence for proximity-dependent mechanosensing. Analogous to our predicted polarization, cells develop coordinated stress fiber alignment with myosin light chain phosphorylation along stress fibers (38), and undergo force- or stretch-dependent polarization (34, 39-43) and spreading (39, 40). Cardiac fibroblasts exhibit calcium influx with $\alpha$-smooth muscle actin upregulation and MRTF-A/MKL1 nuclear translocation in response to paratensile signals (4). Cryptic mechanosensitive sites, such as vinculin-binding sites on talin, undergo force-dependent conformational changes that regulate cytoskeletal dynamics in response to local mechanical fields (44). The Doha et al. study that motivated our work reported these phenomena, with cells in close proximity changing shape markedly and extending protrusions
structures toward each other. Cells extend lateral protrusions to interact with neighbors mechanically (45, 46), with ECM mechanics and cell shape driving stress fiber formation (15), focal adhesion strengthening (47, 48), and myosin regulation (38). Cardiac cells couple only within optimally stiff matrices (42, 49-51).

Our model neglects ECM viscoelasticity, whose timescales (milliseconds to hours) (52-54) overlap with cellular processes: calcium influx ( $<1$ s), cytoskeletal remodeling (seconds to minutes), $\alpha$-SMA upregulation ( $\sim 40 \mathrm{~min}$ ), and transcriptional factor translocation (hours) (4,55,56). Stress relaxation could attenuate mechanical signals, shifting critical spacing. The interplay between force generation and ECM relaxation creates temporal windows for mechanosensing, with viscosity affecting phenotype and spreading (57-60). Active remodeling evolves mechanical properties over days (61), potentially creating time-dependent phase transitions. Future work incorporating viscoelastic models and remodeling kinetics could reveal intervention strategies that exploit the time-dependent nature of cell-ECM interactions.

An important question is whether similar transitions occur in purely elastic or strain-softening ECM. Building on Zemel et al. (13-15), who found gradual changes in linear elastic matrices, our sharp transitions fundamentally require strainstiffening. Without it, positive feedback between contractility, ECM alignment, and local stiffening vanishes. Tension bands specifically require strain-stiffening ECM (20); without them, signals decay following classical elasticity, preventing long-range cell-cell communication coordination. This explains why cells on polyacrylamide behave differently than in collagen despite similar moduli, highlighting strain-stiffening as a design principle enabling collective behaviors.

In conclusion, our bio-chemo-mechanical model demonstrates how cell spacing, shape, and ECM properties control mechanotransduction through fibrous matrices. The critical distance and nonmonotonic stiffness relationship reveal the mechanistic underpinnings of paratensile signaling, with implications for understanding wound healing and fibrosis. Results also underscore the importance of the physical microenvironment in cell-cell communication.

## Materials and Methods

Model of Cell Contractility and Activation. Cells were modeled as actively contracting and adapting ellipsoids embedded in an initially random, fibrous ECM that was very large compared to the cell. Actomyosin contractility was modeled as a directionally distributed field of myosin force dipoles that contract an isotropic background of actin filaments (24). Following Shenoy et al. (25), this was represented by the contractility tensor, $\rho$, derived by integrating forces from myosin dipoles over their orientation distribution. $\boldsymbol{\rho}$ is work-conjugate to the linearized strain tensor, $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, so that the specific work (per unit volume) done by phosphorylated myosin motors is $W=\boldsymbol{\rho}: \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$. Cellular contractility developed in response to tension exposure, resulting in increased F-actin polymerization and elevated levels of phosphorylated myosin light chain (p-MLC), as represented by evolution of $\boldsymbol{\rho}$ evolved with tension (24). The enthalpic state of the cell was represented by five terms (25):

$$
\begin{equation*}
U=U_{\text {elastic }}-W_{\text {ext }}+U_{\text {chem }}-W_{\text {feedback }}+W_{\text {chem }} \tag{1}
\end{equation*}
$$

where the strain energy density is

$$
\begin{equation*}
U_{\text {elastic }}=\frac{K}{2} v^{2}+\mu(\boldsymbol{e}: \boldsymbol{e}) \tag{2}
\end{equation*}
$$

in which $K$ is the cell's bulk modulus, $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ is the infinitesimal strain tensor, $v=\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$ is the trace of $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \mu$ is the shear modulus of the cell, and $\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{3} V I$ is the deviatoric portion of the strain tensor, in which $\boldsymbol{I}$ is the second-order identity tensor. The external specific work was written:

$$
\begin{equation*}
W_{e x t}=\int_{0}^{v} p d v+\int_{0}^{e} s: d e \tag{3}
\end{equation*}
$$

in which $s=\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{p} \boldsymbol{I}$ is the deviatoric component of the linearized stress tensor, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the linearized stress tensor, and $p=\frac{1}{3} \operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})$.

The chemical energy density associated with phosphorylated myosin motors was written:

$$
\begin{equation*}
U_{\text {chem }}=3 \frac{\beta}{2}\left(p_{m}-\rho_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2} \rho^{d e v}: \rho^{d e v} \tag{4}
\end{equation*}
$$

in which $p_{m}=\frac{1}{3} \operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\rho}), \rho_{0}$ is the baseline contractility, $\boldsymbol{\rho}^{d e v}=\boldsymbol{\rho}-p_{m} \boldsymbol{I}$ is the deviatoric component of $\boldsymbol{\rho}$, and $\beta$ is a constant termed the "chemical stiffness." Cells respond to mechanical stress by increasing $\boldsymbol{\rho}$, as governed by the feedback-associated specific work:

$$
\begin{equation*}
W_{\text {feedback }}=3 \alpha \int_{0}^{3 p_{m}} p d p_{m}+\alpha \int_{0}^{\rho^{d e v}} s: d \rho^{d e v} \tag{5}
\end{equation*}
$$

in which the constant $\alpha$ is the chemo-mechanical feedback parameter. Finally, the specific work done by the myosin motors was

$$
\begin{equation*}
W_{\text {chem }}=3 p_{m} v+\rho^{d e v}: \boldsymbol{e} \tag{6}
\end{equation*}
$$

The temporal evolution equations can be inferred by taking time derivatives of Eq. 1 (25). For the cases of interest in which the timescales associated with motor recruitment and activation are short compared to the observation time, the following steady state conditions govern the active cell responses to mechanical stress:

$$
\begin{align*}
& \boldsymbol{\rho}=\left(\bar{\rho}_{0}+3 K^{\rho} v\right) \boldsymbol{I}+2 \mu^{\rho} e,  \tag{7}\\
& \boldsymbol{\sigma}=\left(\bar{\rho}_{0}+3 K^{e} v\right) \boldsymbol{I}+2 \mu^{e} e, \tag{8}
\end{align*}
$$

where $\bar{\rho}_{0}=\gamma \beta \rho_{0}, K^{e}=\gamma(K \beta-1 / 3), \mu^{e}=\gamma(\mu \beta-1 / 2), K^{\rho}= \gamma(K \alpha-1 / 3)$, and $\mu^{\rho}=\gamma(\mu \alpha-1 / 2)$, in which $\gamma=1 /(\beta-\alpha)$.

Model of Fibrous ECM Mechanics. We modeled the fibrous ECM using the approach of Wang et al. (20), which treats the fibrous ECM as a superposition of elastic ground material and a set of fibers that provide additional resistance to deformation after stretching beyond a critical stretch level, $\lambda_{c}$. The total strain energy density of the collagen network is $U=U_{b}+U_{f}$, where the strain energy density associated with the ground material follows a neo-Hookean model:

$$
\begin{equation*}
U_{b}=\frac{\mu^{b}}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2}}{J^{2 / 3}}-3\right)+\frac{K^{b}}{2}(J-1)^{2}, \tag{9}
\end{equation*}
$$

in which $\mu^{b}=E^{b} /\left(2\left(1+v^{b}\right)\right)$ is the initial shear modulus, $K^{b}= E^{b} /\left(3\left(1-2 v^{b}\right)\right)$ is the initial bulk modulus, and $E^{b}$ and $v^{b}$ are the initial elastic modulus and Poisson ratio of the ECM, respectively; $\left\{\lambda_{1}, \lambda_{2}, \lambda_{3}\right\}$ are the principal stretches; and $J=\lambda_{1} \lambda_{2} \lambda_{3}$. The strain energy density associated with aligned fibers is

$$
\begin{equation*}
U_{f}=E^{f} \sum_{i=1}^{3} f\left(\lambda_{i}\right) \tag{10}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $E^{f}$ is a strain-stiffening modulus, and the dimensionless strain-stiffening function $f\left(\lambda_{i}\right)$ captures two fundamental principles: i) the ECM exhibits strain stiffening solely in the direction of the first principal stretch when the stretch ratio exceeds a critical value $\lambda_{c}$, and ii) effects of strain stiffening are negligible for stretch less than $\lambda_{c}$. From this, the stress in the ECM can be written as the sum of the stress in the ground material and the stress in the fiber network, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{E C M}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{b}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{f}$, where (20):

$$
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{b}=K(J-1) \boldsymbol{I}+\mu \boldsymbol{B}^{d e v} / J, \tag{11}
\end{equation*}
$$

in which $\overline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\text {dev }}=\left(\boldsymbol{B}-\frac{1}{3} \operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})\right) / \mathrm{J}^{2 / 3}$ is the deviatoric part of the left modified Cauchy-Green tensor (20). Here, $\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{F F}^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{3} \lambda_{i}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{i} \otimes \boldsymbol{n}_{i}\right)$, where $\boldsymbol{F}$ is the deformation gradient tensor, $\lambda_{i}(i=1,2,3)$ are the principal stretch ratios, and principal directions $\boldsymbol{n}_{i}$ are the unit eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{B}$. Following refs. 20 and 33, the contribution to fiber stiffening was written:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{f}=\frac{E^{f}}{J} \sum_{i=1}^{3} \frac{\partial f\left(\lambda_{i}\right)}{\partial \lambda_{i}} \lambda_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{i} \otimes \boldsymbol{n}_{i}\right), \tag{12}
\end{equation*}
$$

where:

$$
\frac{\partial f\left(\lambda_{i}\right)}{\partial \lambda_{i}}= \begin{cases}0, & \lambda_{i} \leq \lambda_{p}  \tag{13}\\ \frac{\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{p}\right)^{n+1}}{\left(\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{p}\right)^{n}(n+1)^{\prime}} & \lambda_{p} \leq \lambda_{i} \leq \lambda_{q} \\ \frac{\left(1+\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{q}\right)^{m+1}-1}{m+1}+\frac{\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{p}}{n+1}, & \lambda_{i} \geq \lambda_{q}\end{cases}
$$

in which $\lambda_{p} \equiv \lambda_{c}-\lambda_{t} / 2, \lambda_{q} \equiv \lambda_{c}+\lambda_{t} / 2, \lambda_{c}$ is the stretch ratio at which fiber stiffening begins to contribute strongly to ECM mechanics, and $\lambda_{t}$ is a transition region. For and strain-stiffening exponents $m$ and $n$ are fitting parameters $(28,33)$.

Numerical Methods. Equations were solved using the finite element method in the COMSOL Multiphysics environment (COMSOL, Inc., Burlington, MA). Cells were modeled as axisymmetric, and both cells and ECM were discretized with linear triangular elements. Mesh convergence studies were performed, confirming numerical stability and robustness of the results (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). COMSOL's Solid Mechanics interface (part of the Structural Mechanics module) was used to model the mechanical coupling between cells and ECM. Cell responses included both passive and active components: the passive component was modeled as linear hyperelastic, while the active contractile stress, $\rho$, was superimposed on the cell domain. The isotropic ECM response was captured
using a neo-Hookean formulation, and the contribution of aligned fibers was modeled according to Eq. 12. To further support reproducibility, we have made our simulation code publicly available at https://zenodo.org/records/17118632 (62), together with documentation and example scripts.
